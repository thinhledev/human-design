# Colour combinations and domain presets

Checked 27 November 2026. Grades: documented, practice, research, vendor, inference.

The library gives the model what it lacked: sets of colours known to work
together, and what each category of product expects. It is a starting point
for a palette, never the palette. `color.md` section 1 is the method; this
file says what is in the library and how to read it.

## 1. What is in it

- **Figma's 100 combinations** (figma.com/resource-library/color-combinations), in seven groups: monochromatic, romantic, playful, vibrant, neutral, tranquil, seasonal. Each has four colours. The hex codes are the ones printed on Figma's palette images, read on 27 November 2026 and checked against the pixels; one label is malformed (Golden hour's fourth swatch reads `#CC550`) and uses the measured colour instead. Names and groups are Figma's; the tags (calm, technical, appetising and so on) are this skill's reading. Every entry links to its section on Figma's page.
- **18 of this skill's own** (`hd-*`, grade inference) for domains Figma's set leaves thin: dark-ground developer palettes, data-dense finance, clinical health, public services and legal, energy, news, insurance, B2B tools that are not blue, crypto without neon.
- **24 domain presets** (`references/data/domains.json`): developer tools, AI products, B2B SaaS, fintech and banking, crypto, insurance, healthcare, mental health and wellness, fitness, education and language learning, kids, food and restaurants, grocery, fashion, beauty, luxury, home and interiors, travel, real estate, legal and government, climate and energy, media and news, music and gaming, creative tools.

`combinations.md` is the full catalogue, generated by `palette.py --catalogue`:
every combination's colours, harmony, chroma level, temperature, tags and the
domains that list it.

## 2. How a preset is built

Each domain has:

- **Keep**: the convention users rely on (practice unless noted). Developer tools: dark mode as a first-class mode, one signal hue, status colours that mean pass, fail, running. Healthcare: very high legibility, red reserved for real danger.
- **Question**: the category's habit. Fintech: navy, royal blue or a challenger purple. AI: violet gradients and glow.
- **Twist**: where a different answer can come from. Fintech: deep green, brass, oxblood or teal give trust without the house blue.
- **Preferences** for ranking: ground (light, dark, either; dark first or not), temperature, the chroma levels the category reads as fitting, moods to favour and to avoid, and the hue families that are the category's cliché (ranked down a little, never banned).
- **Candidates**: combinations hand-picked as good starting points (ranked up).
- **Evidence**, graded. The research says fit matters (Bottomley and Doyle 2006; Labrecque and Milne 2012). The logo surveys (vendor: Bynder; 99designs as reported by The Drum) say what the category already wears. Most "colour by industry" writing online is opinion; it was not used as evidence.

## 3. How the ranking works

`palette.py` scores every combination:

| Signal | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| listed as a candidate for the domain | strong plus |
| tags shared with the domain's moods, and with the brief's words (`--mood`) | plus for each |
| tags the domain avoids | minus for each |
| chroma level inside the domain's range | plus, else minus |
| temperature and dark-ground fit | small plus or minus |
| place of use (`--place dim`, `bright`, `desk`) | dark grounds for dim rooms; contrast for daylight; no neon for all-day use |
| primary hue family is the category's cliché | small minus |
| primary hue family already leads another of your projects (fingerprint registry) | large minus, named in the output |

The first three results are taken from three different harmonies, so each of
the three directions starts from a different relation. The rest follow by
score. Brief words with no colour meaning (for example "encouraging") are
listed so the model can translate them itself.

## 4. From combination to roles

A combination is four colours, not roles. `palette.py --show ID` shows how
they map, and `--apply ID design.json` writes the result:

- **primary**: the strongest colour that can be a fill for an action (lightness about 0.25 to 0.88);
- **ground**: the lightest colour, tamed to a ground (chroma at most 0.035), or for a dark-first product the darkest; derived from the primary's hue when the set has neither;
- **ink**: the darkest remaining colour, or a dark tint of the ground's hue;
- **secondary**: the strongest remaining colour at least 30 degrees from the primary;
- **highlight**: the next remaining colour; **surface**: a remaining pale tint, when there is one.

`--dark-first` makes the darkest colour the ground and sets the colour modes
to dark first. `--rotate DEG` turns every hue by the same amount, which keeps
the harmony while moving it toward the subject. Then edit the values by hand
from the world study and say why in `palette.source`.

## 5. What it will not do

- It does not decide. A ranked list from a library becomes a house style if taken as it comes; the registry check (`fingerprint.py --against`, `palette.py`'s taken hues) and the twist are what keep projects apart.
- It does not see photographs. When the product has photographs or artwork, sample colours from them first and use the library to find a set that sits with them.
- Its tags and thresholds are judgement (inference). Its research basis is that fit to the category matters and that lightness contrast drives harmony; nothing in it is a measured preference for a specific palette.
- It is not a brand. An existing brand guide wins over every preset (precedence in `SKILL.md`).
